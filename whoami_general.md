# Who Am I — The Framework

A structured, self-guided process for discovering your **values**, your **moral framework**, and your **talents**.

This file is the method. Your answers go in [`whoami_personal.md`](whoami_personal.md), which has the same section numbers, so you can work through both side by side.

---

## How to use this framework

- **Go slowly.** One section per sitting is plenty. Short sessions are fine too: one prompt at a time works. Much of the insight comes in the days between sessions.
- **Stories first, but types are fine.** "I'm loyal" tells you little. "I drove six hours to help a friend move the week I started a new job" tells you a lot. If you think more in patterns than in single memories, a *type* of situation or person ("completing complicated tasks", "the fighter who never gives up") works too. Then ask yourself *why*.
- **Watch what you do, not just what you say.** How you spend time, money, and attention is evidence. When what you claim and what you do disagree, that gap is a finding too.
- **Write it down.** Thinking in your head goes in circles. Writing makes you commit to something.
- **Be honest, not flattering.** Nobody reads this but you (see the privacy note in the README). Write the version that is true, not the version you'd like to be true.
- **Treat everything as a draft.** Date your entries. Come back in a year. Change is expected.

### Get a mirror

Patterns are much easier to see from outside. Some ways to get that:

- Work through it with a **partner or trusted friend** who asks "why?" and reflects back what they notice.
- Use an **AI assistant** as a conversation partner: let it ask one prompt at a time, follow up, and suggest patterns you can accept or push back on. (Think about what you're comfortable sharing with an online service.)
- Working alone: after each section, **re-read your answers as if a stranger wrote them.** What would you conclude about this person?

Pushing back on a suggested pattern is as valuable as accepting one. Your correction is often more accurate than the original guess.

### Take care

These exercises can bring up painful history: old failures, losses, things you envied or missed. That's normal and often where the real insight is. It's also fine to pause, skip a prompt, or work through parts with someone you trust. If something is too big for this session, put it in your **parking lot** (in the personal file) and come back to it.

### Optional lens: how your mind works

If you're neurodivergent (e.g. ADHD, autism), or live with anxiety or other conditions, consider asking along the way: *how might the way my brain works have shaped this answer?* It doesn't explain your values away, but it can explain **how they formed** and **which environments let you live them**.

### Structure

Each section has three parts: **Explore** (raw material), **Distill** (narrow it down), and **Test** (check it against reality). Each one ends with **one next step**.

Sections overlap: an answer about values often reveals something about your morals or talents. When it does, tag it (e.g. "→ section 2") and use it later.

---

## 1. Values

*What matters most to you: the things that make life feel meaningful when they're present and wrong when they're missing.*

### 1.1 Explore

1. **Peak moments.** Think of times you felt:
   - most **proud**
   - most **alive**
   - most **fulfilled**

   For each one: What was happening? Who was there? Then ask *why* it mattered. If it's hard to say, pick from possible reasons. Was it the challenge itself, doing it well, being trusted, the setting, the people, the feeling of being needed?
2. **Frustration and anger.** Write down 3–5 times you were really upset, frustrated, or morally offended. Strong negative reactions usually mean a value was being violated. Which one? Was it your value or someone else's being trampled?
3. **Admiration.** Name up to 3 people (real or fictional) or *types* of people you deeply admire. What specifically do you admire about them?
4. **Envy, past and present.** Whose life or situation have you envied, now or earlier in life? Envy often points to something you value but aren't living. It also hides well: **who annoys or irritates you more than they reasonably should?** That might be envy in disguise.
5. **The ideal ordinary moment.** Describe an ordinary moment (not a vacation, not a big event) when everything feels right. Where are you, who's there, what just happened, what's next?
6. **The eulogy test.** What would you want a close friend to honestly say about you at your funeral?

### 1.2 Distill

1. **Collect.** Go back over your answers and pull out every value they suggest. Use the word list below for help, but your own words are better. Keep a running list of **candidates** in your personal file. You can mark a value **confirmed** as soon as it clearly fits, without waiting for the end.
2. **Group.** Put similar values together (e.g. *freedom, independence, autonomy*). Name each group with the word that feels most like yours.
3. **Value or compensation?** Some things matter because they soothe an old wound rather than because you want them for their own sake (e.g. needing to impress others to quiet a sense of failure). For each candidate ask: **"Would this still matter if nobody saw it?"** Both kinds are worth knowing about; only the first belongs on your values list.
4. **Contradictions.** Look for answers that seem to contradict each other (e.g. "I admire people who sacrifice for others" vs. "I don't want to sacrifice myself"). Work out what makes both true. The answer is usually a sharper definition of a value.
5. **Ends and means.** For each value ask: **"What does this serve? Is there something underneath it?"**
   - **End values** are what you live *for* (e.g. integrity, family).
   - **Means values** are how you get there (e.g. agency, discipline).

   Both can be core. Keep them apart, because means values will lose in a ranking even when they're essential.
6. **Choose.** Narrow to your **top ~5 core values**.
7. **Define.** For each one, write a **personal definition** in one or two sentences. "Freedom" means something different to everyone. What does it mean to *you*?

### 1.3 Test

1. **Forced choice.** For each pair of core values, write a short, concrete scenario where you can only honor one, and pick. Gut answers are fine. "It depends" is allowed, but write down what it depends on. Then count wins and rank. Abstract pairs ("freedom or family?") are hard to answer, but concrete scenarios are easy:

   | # | Scenario | Which wins? |
   |---|---|---|
   | 1 | *A crucial work deadline falls on the same day as an important family event.* | Contribution or Family |
   | 2 | *A close friend is being unfair to someone else, and calling it out could cost you the friendship.* | Fairness or Friendship |
   | … | *one scenario per pair* | |

   Check for loops (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A). If there are any, the definitions probably need work. Remember that means values tend to come last. That doesn't make them unimportant.
2. **Calendar and bank account.** Roughly how does a typical week's time split up? Where does the money you choose to spend go? Which values does that actually serve? Some values are about *how* you act rather than *where* your time goes (e.g. integrity, fairness). Test those differently: **when did you last act on this value when it cost you something?**
3. **How it feels.** Which part of your week feels most like *you*? Which feels like a waste? **What feels worst even though you need it?**
4. **Gap list.** Do this last, since the earlier steps will already have uncovered some gaps. Where does your life go against your core values? For each gap:
   - Is it a **knowing gap** (you don't know what to do) or a **doing gap** (you know, but haven't done it)?
   - **What has kept you from closing it?** The answer often reveals a value you missed, or one that others are using as a lever (e.g. loyalty). Add new values back to your list.

### 1.4 Next step

Pick **one gap** and turn it into a concrete step with an **end date** and **conditions** ("by 31 March, if X hasn't happened, I do Y"). Put the date in your actual calendar.

## 2. Moral framework

*How you decide what is right and wrong: your intuitions, your reasoning, and the lines you won't cross.*

If you tagged answers "→ section 2" while working on values, start by collecting them here.

### 2.1 Explore: your moral intuitions

Moral psychology (e.g. Jonathan Haidt's *Moral Foundations Theory*) suggests people rely on several basic intuitions to different degrees. Rate how strongly each one drives your moral judgments (1 = barely, 5 = very strongly), and write down an example of when it showed up for you:

| Foundation | What it's about |
|---|---|
| **Care / harm** | Protecting others from suffering |
| **Fairness / cheating** | Justice, reciprocity, equal treatment |
| **Liberty / oppression** | Freedom from domination and control |
| **Loyalty / betrayal** | Standing by your group, family, team |
| **Authority / subversion** | Respect for roles, tradition, legitimate order |
| **Sanctity / degradation** | Purity, the sacred, things that shouldn't be defiled |

### 2.2 Explore: how you reason

When you face a hard moral decision, which of these questions do you actually reach for first?

- **Consequences:** *What will produce the best outcome for everyone affected?*
- **Duties and rules:** *What are my obligations? What rule applies? Would it be OK if everyone did this?*
- **Character:** *What would a good person, the person I want to be, do here?*
- **Relationships and care:** *What does this particular relationship need from me?*

Work through 3 real dilemmas from your life (ones you faced, or still face). For each one, write down what you did or would do, and *why*. Then name which lens(es) you used.

### 2.3 Explore: boundaries and growth

1. **Hard lines.** What would you refuse to do even for a lot of money, status, or to protect yourself?
2. **Grey zones.** Where do you knowingly bend your own rules? Why do you allow it there?
3. **What "fair" means to you.** Equal outcomes for everyone? Equal opportunity, with reward in proportion to effort? Something else? Give an example.
4. **The limits of loyalty.** Who are you loyal to, and what would end it? Has anyone used your loyalty as a lever?
5. **The act and the person.** When someone does wrong, do you judge the act, the person, or both? Do you want punishment, repair, or resolution? Do you hold grudges?
6. **Changed mind.** Name a moral view you've changed. What changed it?
7. **Moral circle.** Whose interests count in your decisions: family, friends, strangers, future generations, animals, nature? Where does the circle get thinner?
8. **Source.** Where do your morals come from: upbringing, religion, philosophy, experience, reason? Which of those have you examined, and which have you just inherited?

### 2.4 Distill

Write your **personal moral code**: 5–10 short statements you actually live by, or want to live by. Mark which ones you already live and which you're aiming for.

### 2.5 Test

1. Show your code to someone who knows you well. Ask: "Does this sound like me? Where don't I live up to it?"
2. Check it against your core values (section 1). Do they conflict anywhere? If so, how do you settle it?

### 2.6 Next step

Pick **one** place where you don't live up to your code, and set a concrete step with an end date.

## 3. Talents

*Your natural patterns of thinking, feeling, and behaving that can be put to productive use. Talents are raw ability. Skills are learned. Strengths are talents developed with skill and practice.*

If you tagged answers "→ section 3" earlier, start by collecting them here.

### 3.1 Explore

1. **Easy for you, hard for others.** What do people find difficult that you find surprisingly easy? (These are easy to miss, because they feel ordinary to you.)
2. **Energy audit.** For one to two weeks, note which activities left you energized and which drained you, even if you're good at both.
3. **Good stress vs. wrong stress.** What kind of pressure brings out your best, and what kind wears you down? Look at control, fairness, clarity, the people involved, and the type of challenge.

   | Good stress | Wrong stress |
   |---|---|
   | *e.g. hard challenge, I'm in control* | *e.g. chaos, other people's mess lands on me* |
4. **Flow.** When do you lose track of time? What are you doing?
5. **Fast learning.** What have you picked up much faster than expected?
6. **Investing in yourself.** What work do you do to get better at doing things in the future (learning, tools, practice)? What does that say about what you're building?
7. **Childhood clues.** What did you do for hours as a kid without being told to?
8. **What people ask you for.** What do friends and colleagues come to you for: advice, help, opinions?
9. **Outside view.** Ask 3–5 people who know you in different settings: *"What do you think I'm unusually good at?"* and *"When have you seen me at my best?"* Write their answers down word for word.

### 3.2 Distill

1. Look for patterns across all your answers, especially ones that showed up in more than one exercise.
2. Name your **top 3–5 talents**. Use verbs and specifics ("making complex things simple for others", not "communication").
3. For each one, write down:
   - **Evidence:** 2–3 concrete examples
   - **Shadow side:** how this talent causes trouble when overused (e.g. a talent for analysis turns into overthinking, and high endurance turns into ignoring warning signs)
   - **Development:** what skill or practice would turn it into a real strength

### 3.3 Test

1. Where in your current life do you use these talents? Where do they go unused?
2. Which tasks fight your natural talents? Can any of them be dropped, delegated, or redesigned?
3. Does your current environment give you more good stress or more wrong stress?

### 3.4 Next step

Pick **one** change that lets you use a talent more (or fight it less), with an end date.

---

## 4. Synthesis

Bring the sections together.

1. **Connections.** How do your values, moral code, and talents fit together? Where do they reinforce each other? Where do they pull against each other?
2. **Personal statement.** In one paragraph: who you are, what you stand for, and what you bring.
3. **Next steps.** Collect the next steps from each section. Are they still the right ones? Do they have dates?
4. **Parking lot.** Look at what you parked. Which item deserves its own session, or its own module?
5. **Revisit date.** Pick a date (6–12 months from now) to go through the framework again.

---

## 5. Optional modules

The framework is meant to grow. Possible future modules:

- **Relationships:** how you connect, what you need from others, and feeling accepted as you are
- **Interests and passions:** what pulls your curiosity
- **Motivation:** what drives you (and what demotivates you)
- **Health and energy:** sleep, stress, recovery, and what rest actually looks like for you
- **Purpose:** what you want to contribute beyond yourself
- **Fears and blind spots:** what holds you back

### Adding a module

To add a module, give it the next section number and use the same structure:

```markdown
## N. Module name

*One-sentence description of what this module discovers.*

### N.1 Explore
Story- and evidence-based prompts. Accept both specific stories and types of situations.

### N.2 Distill
Narrow the raw material into a short, named set of conclusions.

### N.3 Test
Check the conclusions against real behavior and other people's views.

### N.4 Next step
One concrete step with an end date.
```

Then add the matching empty section to `whoami_personal.md`.
